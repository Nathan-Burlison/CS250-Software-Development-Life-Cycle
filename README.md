This reflection summarizes what I learned about using Scrum practices to turn customer requests into testable features, respond to changing priorities, and improve collaboration. The SNHU Travel project was a course simulation in which I worked through Product Owner, developer, tester, and Scrum Master responsibilities. It was not a deployed application.

Reflection

How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I start by figuring out what the user is trying to accomplish, rather than focusing only on the feature they request. Then I break that need into specific behaviors that can be developed and tested. In the SNHU Travel project, I worked with requests for personalized destination recommendations, a maximum-price filter, and saved cruise preferences. User stories helped explain each feature from the customer's point of view, while acceptance criteria made the expectations measurable. For example, the price filter had to handle valid prices, invalid input, and searches with no matching results. Thinking through those situations helped me see how a simple request can involve several details that matter to the user.

How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

My approach is to understand the requirements, break the work into manageable tasks, develop one part at a time, and test as I go. I also want to leave room for feedback because requirements can change after development starts. In the SNHU Travel exercises, a change to the slideshow requirements showed me why it is important to clarify priorities and update acceptance criteria before making changes. In future projects, I hope to use a prioritized backlog, Sprint Planning, regular team check-ins, Sprint Reviews, and Retrospectives. I also want to use a clear Definition of Done so testing and quality do not get overlooked when schedules or priorities change.

What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Being a good team member means doing my part, communicating clearly, and helping the team solve problems instead of working in isolation. It also means being willing to ask questions when something is unclear. During the SNHU Travel project, I learned how important it is to confirm changing priorities with the Product Owner and discuss possible problems with testers, such as what should happen when an image fails to load or a user reaches the end of a slideshow. I want teammates to be able to depend on my work and trust that I will raise issues early, accept feedback, and follow through on improvements. A successful software team shares responsibility for the finished product, not just for individual assignments.
